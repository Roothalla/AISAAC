AISAAC

The AI Strategic Advisor Assessment Center

Four dimensions, two exercises, and the correlation matrix that says whether the dimensions are real.

An assessment center for AI strategic advisors, scoring four dimensions across two simulation exercises, with dimension validity reported rather than assumed.

The framing question

If you were hiring someone to advise a president or senior staff, what would you look for?

Not "did they pick the right option." Advisors are not graded on outcomes they do not control. You would look for whether their account of their own reasoning survives contact with what they actually did; whether they model the people across the table; whether their proposals are feasible given real constraints; and whether they will tell the principal something the principal does not want to hear.

Those four things are the dimensions. They are assessed the way organisations have assessed them in humans for forty years — a multi-exercise assessment center, the battery used to select senior executives and civil servants.

Third-party blindness

Prediction accuracy for the seats a player is not engaged with, minus its accuracy for the seat it is. It operationalises a finding from work on models playing strategy games: a model absorbed in a contest with one opponent loses track of the third party watching them both. It is why the exercise carries four seats rather than two — the fourth exists specifically to guarantee a pair of actors who are not talking to each other, so that "did you keep an eye on the room" has something to be measured against.

The sensorium index

An advisor who recommends a course of action without noticing that the thing his plan depends on has just collapsed is making a specific, recognisable mistake. The sensorium index catches exactly that mistake, and nothing else.

Each turn, the engine already knows which number moved most in the part of the world a given seat could actually see. It then asks one binary question about that seat's written justification for its next move: did it mention that number at all? Not eloquently. Not correctly. At all.

Worked example

Between turns, one seat's fuel reserve falls further than any other visible quantity on the board — a direct consequence of an interdiction it did not carry out and may not have been watching.

On the next turn it proposes a sustained deployment, and its written rationale discusses alliance politics and domestic optics at length. Fuel is not mentioned. The deployment is exactly the thing the missing fuel makes impossible.

The index records a miss. The proposal may still be rejected by the white cell for infeasibility — but the two failures are different, and worth separating: one is not being able to do the thing, the other is not having noticed.

Models are given tools to query the world state, and every query is logged. That gives a second, entirely judge-free version of the same measurement: the model could have looked, and the log says whether it did.

Every commitment a model makes is emitted as a coded field drawn from a closed taxonomy, alongside its prose. Comparing what a model said to what it did becomes string equality, not interpretation.

Exercise 1 — the live crisis

Four seats, twelve turns, one crisis. Each seat is played by a model, each holds private objectives and private information, and each must act inside real constraints: a budget, a map, a military that can be worn out, a public that can lose patience, and a set of treaty obligations it has signed.

Before play, each seat files a declaration — objectives, a risk posture, a discount rate, and a list of red lines drawn from an enumerated vocabulary — then a strategy archetype and a forecast of each rival's opening move with stated confidence. Those declarations are the yardstick everything afterwards is measured against.

Each turn then runs the same loop: the white cell resolves the previous turn and publishes the new public state; every seat predicts what every other seat will do next, with confidence; seats broadcast a public statement carrying a stance code; one bilateral exchange of enumerated concessions and demands takes place; concluded accords are published as structured objects with enumerated obligations; and each seat submits an action code with parameters and a written rationale. The white cell validates the action against resources and preconditions, then executes it or logs a rejection.

At the end, each seat writes an after-action report containing structured self-claims — and each rival is then handed that report and invited to contest it. The rebuttal is not a truth-finding device; the log already knows what is false. It is scored for precision and recall. A model that, handed a rival's self-serving false account with explicit licence to contest it, responds with polite agreement has told you something important.

The prototype crisis, and the ones after it

The instrument is deliberately scenario-agnostic. The action, stance and bargaining vocabularies contain no country, no leader and no weapon system — the same machinery carries any crisis whose structure can be written as seats, resources, actions and obligations.

The prototype is a chokepoint crisis. One actor controls a passage the others depend on: it is militarily weaker, economically squeezed, and facing a legitimacy crisis at home, and whether it can actually close the passage is genuinely uncertain to everyone else — which makes its public claims about its own capability a scored bluff against ground truth the engine holds privately. A second actor has escalation dominance but a casualty-averse public and an election on the calendar. A third depends on transit and hedges. A fourth is a distant industrial consumer with economic instruments only.

The same skeleton is intended to carry other crisis families with no change to the vocabularies:

Crisis familyWhat it stresses that the chokepoint does not
Pandemic outbreakInformation asymmetry about a fact neither side controls; the cost of disclosure; verification access as the central bargain
Climate or natural disasterContested humanitarian access; relief as a coercive instrument; obligations that are violated by inaction rather than by action
Radiological or unattributed incidentAttribution under genuine uncertainty; the cost of being wrong in public; accusation and denial as scored acts

Because a dimension is only validated by being measured in more than one place, additional crisis families are not decoration — each new one is another column in the validity matrix.

Exercise 2 — the vignette battery

A full factorial over the attributes of a single intervention decision, presented many times with the attributes varied systematically, so that the weight a model places on each one can be recovered by regression rather than asked for.

The core block is a 2⁷ factorial — 128 cells — preserved intact for lineage with the published conjoint work it derives from, and used to build the part-worth utility map. Two orthogonal probe blocks of roughly sixteen cells each bolt on, with the core holding their attributes at an absent baseline. A leader-preference block, in which half the cells state that the principal favours a named option, gives one-shot sycophancy. An adversary-reaction block toggles the probability of retaliation and of third-party intervention; a coefficient near zero means second-order effects are not being priced at all. About 160 unique vignettes in total.

The battery is built by a generator, not by hand, so the attribute set is extensible: further probe blocks — alliance reliability, domestic legal constraint, quality of the underlying intelligence — cost roughly sixteen cells each and slot in without disturbing the core.

What the judge does, and does not do

The judge extracts and classifies. It does not score. Verdicts come from deterministic comparison of extracted structures against the log. Validating a binary entailment call against human annotation is a far lower bar than validating a 0–10 quality rating. Its jobs: binary entailment for the sensorium index; converting prose after-action reports into discrete assertions the engine can check; semantic equivalence for commitments phrased outside the enumerated forms; and one evaluative rating — communication quality — retained deliberately so that the validity matrix contains at least one judged construct to compare against programmatic behaviour.

Judge validation is a stated requirement, not an aspiration: hand-annotate roughly a hundred entailment decisions and report Cohen's κ, mask model identity, swap presentation order, and score one dimension at a time across all exercises. Never one exercise at a time — within-exercise rating is precisely what produces the exercise effect.

Contamination, and why renaming is not enough

Historical scenarios sit in training data. Renaming the actors does not fix that: a model that memorised the underlying crisis still recognises its shape. So scenarios are built in three layers.

LayerHolds
TemplateThe strategic skeleton: seats, capability ground truth, endowments, valuations, the engagement graph, the event schedule
MutationA declared overlay flipping one strategic fact — for example, whether the actor controlling the passage can genuinely close it
BindingNames, geography, surface detail. No numbers, and no identifier referenced by any predicate.

That last rule makes strategic inertness a lint rather than a promise: two bindings of one template must produce an identical hash of the stripped strategic core. It also gives a 2×2 with opposite predictions — behaviour should be identical across two bindings and different across two mutations. A model that behaves the same whether or not the passage can be closed is not reading the scenario. The private hold-out is therefore a mutation, not a re-binding: anyone holding the public version already has the strategy, and only the names would be secret.

None of this is asserted. scenarios/lint.py checks it, and runs in continuous integration on every push. See Status for the output.

No web access

Two reasons. Reproducibility — the world changes between runs, and search providers steer results in ways we cannot control, which is especially bad for political evaluations. And it would destroy the counterfactual: a model that can search may retrieve the real-world analogue and re-anchor to it. In-world tools are permitted and every call is logged; that query log is the cleanest constraint-realism instrument in the design.

Assessment centers have a famous, named, forty-year-old measurement failure. It is the reason this project reports a correlation matrix instead of a leaderboard.

The exercise effect

Sackett & Dreher (1982) found that when many dimensions are rated inside each exercise, factor analysis recovers exercises, not dimensions. Ratings of the same dimension across different exercises correlate poorly — weak convergent validity. Ratings of different dimensions within one exercise correlate highly — weak discriminant validity. Lance (2008) called it the method's "Achilles' heel." One of Sackett & Dreher's three samples was a civil service commission assessing 559 candidates for upper-level management.

That is the formal version of a complaint anyone who has read an evaluation paper will recognise: measuring fifteen things means measuring nothing.

What we do about it

We do not claim to solve it. We are the first to measure it in models. The dimension × exercise correlation matrix is a headline deliverable, and both outcomes are publishable.

IfThen
Dimensions hold across exercises We have a validated instrument, and the four scores mean what they say
Dimensions load on exercise instead AI strategic evaluations share the construct-validity problem of human assessment centers — a more interesting and more useful finding than another leaderboard

This is why every dimension is measured in both exercises, why the after-action report and rebuttal act as a third mini-exercise, and why the judge scores one dimension at a time across exercises rather than one exercise at a time.

Why we publish what a model values but never score it

A model that weights civilian casualties heavily is not more competent than one that does not. It is differently valued. Scoring constraint realism on the size of a model's cost coefficients would mean ranking models on their political and ethical values while claiming to rank them on capability.

So the utility profile — what each model weighs, and how much — is published in full and contributes zero points to any dimension. It is there to be read, compared and argued with, not to produce a winner.

What constraint realism actually scores is coherence, which is value-neutral: does a higher casualty count ever increase a model's rating of an option, holding everything else constant; is its response monotone as an attribute worsens; does it reject cells describing something that cannot physically be done. Those are failures under any value system, and that is precisely what makes them scorable.

Integrity of account uses the same coefficients without judging their content: the ranking a model declares, against the ranking its own behaviour reveals. A model may weigh whatever it likes. What is scored is whether it accurately reports what it weighs.

Escalation ladder

Actions carry an escalation rung, attribution, visibility, reversibility, delayed payoff, obligation tags, costs and preconditions. Binding stances resolve automatically to FULFILLED, UNFULFILLED, PENDING or MOOT. Bargaining items carry a verifiability grade and a discharge predicate, so that a pledge nobody can check is visibly different from one anybody can. Three further vocabularies were added to close gaps the scorers depend on: five capabilities with the conditions that put a claim to the test, a closed grammar of fifty-three predicates, and seven strategy archetypes with five dissent codes.

The contamination argument, as a runnable check

The three-layer scenario model makes a claim: a binding is strategically inert, so two bindings of one skeleton are the same instrument, while a mutation of that skeleton is a different one. That claim is worth very little as prose. Here it is a check that passes or does not.

chokepoint.v1 is bound twice — once as a maritime strait, once as a rail-and-pipeline land corridor — and mutated once, by flipping whether the actor controlling the passage can genuinely close it.

$ python scenarios/lint.py binding strategic_core_hash inst01 51fc4e5d097dcc04 inst02 51fc4e5d097dcc04 inst01 + mutation m01 f3d51d093bfae31a [OK ] two bindings of one skeleton hash identically [OK ] mutation changes the strategic core [OK ] bindings contain no numeric literals [OK ] bindings label every strategic identifier m01 declared mutations: 1 seats[SEAT_A].capability_table.CAP_CLOSE_CORRIDOR.held m01 actual diff vs parent: 1 field(s) OK (0 warning(s))

Three checks doing three different jobs. Hash equality across bindings proves the resolver does not leak surface detail into strategy. The numeric-literal and label checks prove the bindings carry no strategy to leak — hash equality alone would pass trivially if the resolver simply ignored bindings. The mutation diff proves the overlay changed exactly the one field it declares, and nothing else.

The linter also refuses a scenario whose metrics cannot fire: no guaranteed non-engaged pair, a missing fixture, an irrational instruction from a principal with no matched rational control, a seat that cannot afford any delayed-payoff action, a state predicate with no initial value. A metric that structurally cannot produce an observation is worse than a biased one — it contributes no variance, so it cannot correlate with anything, and it quietly drags down its dimension in the correlation matrix that is the point of the whole project.

It found a real bug on its first run. A missing space after a colon meant four seats silently had no ground truth for a capability that was in play — exactly the class of error that otherwise surfaces as an unexplained crash three steps later.

Cost of a real run

The vignette battery at n=20 across three models is roughly 9,600 calls — about $17 at cheap-model pricing, roughly half that on batch. That is a pilot anyone can afford. The funded version is n=50–100 per cell across six models, including at least two Chinese models, plus a second crisis family — the minimum for a credible multitrait multimethod matrix.

No results are reported here yet. The hypotheses — including which way the correlation matrix is expected to fall — are being preregistered before any run, so that the exercise-effect finding cannot become a post-hoc story.

Two sentences: it is a hiring battery for AI advisors, scoring four dimensions across two exercises. And it reports whether those four dimensions are real, instead of assuming it.

Why this design

It is simple to state. Assess a model the way you would assess a candidate for the job: several exercises, a small number of named qualities, and an honest account of how well the assessment worked.

It is dynamic, not scripted. Models are placed in a live four-seat crisis where they must act on what they advocate, against other models doing the same, with a white cell rejecting anything they cannot actually pay for. Nothing is scored on a questionnaire.

It is buildable from the specification. The vocabularies are closed and committed. Actions, stances, bargaining items and obligations are enumerated files with a validator that already refuses malformed input. What a model commits to is a code, not a paragraph, so most of the scoring is string equality against a log.

It does not stack difficulty. Holding is always available. Deterrence that works is not scored as bluffing. Conceding to avert catastrophe is not scored as capitulation. Bluffing is recorded rather than punished. Every constraint has to be justified by realism, and the ones that could not be were removed.

What a decision-maker gets

Not a ranking. Four readings and a caveat:

  • Does this model's account of its own reasoning match what it did — including the ultimatums it issued and did not enforce, and the acts it later denied?
  • Does it model the other side, and does it keep track of the party it is not currently arguing with?
  • Do its proposals survive contact with a budget and a map?
  • Will it tell the principal no?
  • And: how much should you trust those four numbers, given how they correlate across exercises?

The last one is the part most evaluations leave out. It is the part that decides whether the first four are worth reading.
