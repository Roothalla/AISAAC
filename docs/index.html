Evals for the Situation Room

Advisor Assessment Center

Four dimensions, two exercises, and the correlation matrix that says whether the dimensions are real.

An assessment center for AI strategic advisors, scoring four dimensions across two simulation exercises, with dimension validity reported rather than assumed.

The framing question

If you were hiring someone to advise a president or senior staff, what would you look for?

Not "did they pick the right option." Advisors are not graded on outcomes they do not control. You would look for whether their account of their own reasoning survives contact with what they actually did; whether they model the people across the table; whether their proposals are feasible given real constraints; and whether they will tell the principal something the principal does not want to hear.

Those four things are the dimensions. They are assessed the way organisations have assessed them in humans for forty years — a multi-exercise assessment center, the battery used to select senior executives and civil servants.

The four dimensions

Each is measured in both exercises. A dimension measured in only one exercise cannot be validated at all.

Third-party blindness

Prediction accuracy for the seats a player is not engaged with, minus its accuracy for the seat it is. It operationalises a finding from work on models playing strategy games: a model absorbed in a contest with one opponent loses track of the third party watching them both. It is the most novel single metric in the design, and it is why the scenario carries four seats rather than two — the fourth exists to guarantee a clean non-engaged pair.

The sensorium index

Each turn, the engine knows which state variable moved most in the part of the world a seat could see. The index asks whether the seat's stated rationale references it. The point is not eloquence; it is whether the advisor is looking at the board. A parallel, judge-free version reads the model's tool-query log instead: the finding only exists as a measurement because the model could have looked and did not.

Every commitment a model makes is emitted as a coded field drawn from a closed taxonomy, alongside its prose. Comparing what a model said to what it did becomes string equality, not interpretation.

Exercise 1 — the wargame

Four seats, eight turns, a chokepoint crisis. Before play, each seat returns a declaration memo: objectives, a risk posture ρ, a discount rate γ, and a list of red lines drawn from the enumerated bargaining vocabulary. Then a strategy archetype, attribute weights, and a forecast of each rival's opening move with stated confidence.

Each turn: the white cell resolves the previous turn and publishes the new public state; every seat predicts every other seat's next action with confidence; seats broadcast a public statement carrying a stance code; one bilateral exchange of enumerated concessions and demands; concluded accords are broadcast as structured objects with enumerated obligations; and each seat submits an action code with parameters and a rationale. The white cell validates against resource and transit constraints, then executes or logs a rejection.

At the end, an after-action report with structured self-claims — then each rival is handed that report and invited to contest it. The rebuttal is not a truth-finding device; the log already knows what is false. It is scored for precision and recall. A model that, handed a rival's self-serving false account with explicit licence to contest it, responds with polite agreement has told you something important.

Exercise 2 — the vignette battery

A 2⁷ full factorial as the core block, preserved intact for the part-worth utility map, with two orthogonal probe blocks bolted on. A leader-preference block, where half the cells state that the principal favours a named option, gives one-shot sycophancy. An adversary-reaction block toggles the probability of retaliation and of third-party intervention; a coefficient near zero means second-order effects are not being priced. About 160 unique vignettes.

What the judge does, and does not do

The judge extracts and classifies. It does not score. Verdicts come from deterministic comparison of extracted structures against the log. Validating a binary entailment call against human annotation is a far lower bar than validating a 0–10 quality rating. Its jobs: binary entailment for the sensorium index; converting prose after-action reports into discrete assertions the engine can check; semantic equivalence for commitments phrased outside the enumerated forms; and one evaluative rating — communication quality — retained deliberately so the validity matrix contains at least one judged construct to compare against programmatic behaviour.

Judge validation is a stated requirement, not an aspiration: hand-annotate roughly a hundred entailment decisions and report Cohen's κ, mask model identity, swap presentation order, and score one dimension at a time across all exercises. Never one exercise at a time — within-exercise rating is precisely what produces the exercise effect.

Contamination, and why renaming is not enough

Historical scenarios sit in training data. Renaming the actors does not fix it: a model that memorised the underlying crisis still recognises its shape. So scenarios are built in three layers.

LayerHolds
TemplateThe strategic skeleton: seats, capability ground truth, endowments, valuations, the engagement graph, the event schedule
MutationA declared overlay flipping one strategic fact — for example, whether the seat controlling the passage can actually close it
BindingNames, geography, surface detail. No numbers, and no identifier referenced by any predicate.

That last rule makes strategic inertness a lint rather than a promise: two bindings of one template must produce an identical hash of the stripped strategic core. It also gives a 2×2 with opposite predictions — behaviour should be identical across two bindings and different across two mutations. A model that behaves the same whether or not the chokepoint can be closed is not reading the scenario. The private hold-out is a mutation, not a re-binding: anyone holding the public version already has the strategy, and only the names would be secret.

No web access

Two reasons. Reproducibility — the world changes between runs and search providers steer results in ways we cannot control, which is especially bad for political evals. And it would destroy the counterfactual: a model that can search may retrieve the real-world analogue and re-anchor to it. In-world tools are permitted and every call is logged; the query log is the cleanest constraint-realism instrument we have.

Assessment centers have a famous, named, forty-year-old measurement failure. It is the reason this project reports a correlation matrix instead of a leaderboard.

The exercise effect

Sackett & Dreher (1982) found that when many dimensions are rated inside each exercise, factor analysis recovers exercises, not dimensions. Ratings of the same dimension across different exercises correlate poorly — weak convergent validity. Ratings of different dimensions within one exercise correlate highly — weak discriminant validity. Lance (2008) called it the method's "Achilles' heel." One of Sackett & Dreher's three samples was a civil service commission assessing 559 candidates for upper-level management.

That is the formal version of a complaint anyone who has read an eval paper will recognise: measuring fifteen things means measuring nothing.

What we do about it

We do not claim to solve it. We are the first to measure it in models. The dimension × exercise correlation matrix is a headline deliverable, and both outcomes are publishable.

IfThen
Dimensions hold across exercises We have a validated instrument, and the four scores mean what they say
Dimensions load on exercise instead AI strategic evals share the construct-validity problem of human assessment centers — a more interesting and more useful finding than another leaderboard

This is why every dimension is measured in both exercises, why the after-action report and rebuttal act as a third mini-exercise, and why the judge scores one dimension at a time across exercises rather than one exercise at a time.

The trap we refuse

Constraint realism is never built from the magnitude of cost coefficients. A model that weights civilian casualties heavily is not more competent — it is differently valued. Scoring on magnitude means ranking models on political and ethical values while claiming to rank them on capability.

So the utility profile is published and unscored. It answers "what does this model weigh," and it contributes zero points to any dimension. D3 uses only coherence properties: does a higher casualty count ever increase the score holding all else constant; is the response monotone across the attribute; are infeasible trap cells rejected. Those are failures under any value system, which is exactly what makes them scorable.

D1 uses the same coefficients without judging their content: declared ranking against empirical ranking. A model may weigh whatever it likes. What is scored is whether it accurately reports what it weighs.

Cost of a real run

The vignette battery at n=20 across three models is roughly 9,600 calls — about $17 at cheap-model pricing, roughly half that on batch. That is a pilot anyone can afford. The funded version is n=50–100 per cell across six models, including at least two Chinese models, plus two wargame scenarios — the minimum for a credible multitrait multimethod matrix.

No results are reported here yet. The hypotheses — including which way the correlation matrix is expected to fall — are being preregistered before any run, so that the exercise-effect finding cannot be a post-hoc story.

Two sentences: it is a hiring battery for AI advisors, scoring four dimensions across two exercises. And it reports whether those four dimensions are real, instead of assuming it.

Why this design

It is simple to state. Assess a model the way you would assess a candidate for the job: several exercises, a small number of named qualities, and an honest account of how well the assessment worked.

It is dynamic, not scripted. Models are placed in a live four-seat crisis where they must act on what they advocate, against other models doing the same, with a white cell rejecting anything they cannot actually pay for. Nothing is scored on a questionnaire.

It is buildable from the specification. The vocabularies are closed and committed. Actions, stances, bargaining items and obligations are enumerated files with a validator that already refuses malformed input. What a model commits to is a code, not a paragraph, so most of the scoring is string equality against a log.

It does not stack difficulty. Holding is always available. Deterrence that works is not scored as bluffing. Conceding to avert catastrophe is not scored as capitulation. Bluffing is recorded rather than punished. Every constraint has to be justified by realism, and the ones that could not be were removed.

What a decision-maker gets

Not a ranking. Four readings and a caveat:

  • Does this model's account of its own reasoning match what it did — including the ultimatums it issued and did not enforce, and the acts it later denied?
  • Does it model the other side, and does it keep track of the party it is not currently arguing with?
  • Do its proposals survive contact with a budget and a map?
  • Will it tell the principal no?
  • And: how much should you trust those four numbers, given how they correlate across exercises?

The last one is the part most evals leave out. It is the part that decides whether the first four are worth reading.
